# Anonymized Example: From a Narrow Carbon-Data Topic to a Governance Proposal

This example is deliberately anonymized and simplified. Company names, exact values, filenames, dates, and identifying advisor details have been removed or generalized.

## Initial situation

A professional-accounting student identified a global electronics manufacturer that had substantially restated its value-chain greenhouse-gas emissions after changes in organizational coverage and calculation boundaries.

The initial topic focused on:

> The effect of boundary changes on the comparability of Scope 3 emissions disclosure.

The case had useful public data, but the proposed conclusion was likely to remain at the level of “the company disclosed a restatement but did not fully explain every component.” The advisor considered the topic too narrow for a full master's paper and suggested treating the restatement as one point within a broader study of carbon governance, strategy, operations, and international expansion.

## Candidate correction

The revised design did not simply lengthen the title. It changed the level of analysis:

- **Research object**: supply-chain carbon-governance mechanism;
- **technical axis**: Scope 3 accounting boundary and data governance;
- **organizational axis**: supplier admission, data reporting, audit, remediation, and capacity building;
- **institutional setting**: international low-carbon rules and publicly stated customer requirements;
- **result boundary**: support for customer response, overseas compliance preparation, and supply-chain coordination, without claiming proven export growth.

## P0 result

The revised topic passed P0 because:

- the problem connected management control, non-financial information, and supply-chain governance;
- the company played both supplier and supply-chain coordinator roles;
- the case contained a visible longitudinal governance process;
- the topic responded to an advisor-identified strategic and operational question.

## P1 test

The validation assembled:

- several years of sustainability reports;
- governance and supplier-management documents;
- official carbon-accounting standards and regulatory materials;
- a table separating original, restated, and subsequent emissions values;
- evidence of carbon-data systems, supplier requirements, review, and remediation;
- a verified literature set covering management control, Scope 3 accounting, supply-chain governance, and global-value-chain pressure.

A pilot analysis showed that the boundary restatement could be used as evidence of data-governance change, but public evidence could not decompose every numerical change or prove that carbon governance caused overseas revenue growth. The final question was narrowed accordingly.

## Final question chain

### Main question

How does a global manufacturer internalize international low-carbon rules and public customer requirements through management controls, use Scope 3 data governance to support supplier governance, and form a supply-chain carbon-governance mechanism for global operations?

### Subquestions

1. How are international low-carbon rules, public customer requirements, and global operating needs translated into organizational responsibilities, targets, internal carbon pricing, and data systems?
2. How are Scope 3 boundaries, supplier activity data, emission factors, base-year restatements, review, and assurance organized into a data-governance process?
3. How does Scope 3 data governance connect with supplier admission, audit, remediation, and capacity building, and what support for global operations can the public evidence establish?

The original pressure and internalization questions were combined because they formed one continuous mechanism and relied on overlapping evidence. This created a cleaner three-question chain without removing the external-pressure evidence.

## Chapter architecture

- **Chapter 3: Case introduction.** Institutional setting, company profile, global footprint, dual role in the supply chain, carbon-governance history, and emissions structure.
- **Chapter 4: External requirements and organizational internalization.** Answers subquestion 1.
- **Chapter 5: Scope 3 data governance.** Answers subquestion 2 and retains the earlier boundary-restatement topic as a full technical chapter.
- **Chapter 6: Supplier governance and global operations.** Answers subquestion 3 using supplier admission, review, remediation, and capacity-building evidence.
- **Chapter 7: Conclusions and implications.** Summarizes the mechanism, boundaries, and implications for comparable global supply-chain firms.

The framework diagram was revised to mirror this order. The original diagram was retained, and the new editable version separated the case-introduction block from the three analysis blocks.

## Methods

The final design used the method names accepted by the program and advisor:

- case analysis to reconstruct the longitudinal governance process and connect the three analytical stages;
- text analysis to code company, supplier, assurance, customer, and policy documents.

Time-series tables, peer references, calculation bridges, and evidence triangulation were treated as analytical operations rather than separate methods. An event study was removed because capital-market reaction was not part of the central question.

## Writing correction

The proposal replaced strong statements such as “carbon governance promotes international expansion” with bounded language such as “provides information and management support for customer response and overseas compliance preparation.” It also distinguished public customer requirements from unverified claims about private procurement decisions.

## Transferable lessons

1. A narrow measurement issue can become a useful technical axis without becoming the entire thesis.
2. Advisor feedback about scope requires redesigning the analytical level, not merely rewriting the title.
3. Public case evidence can support process and mechanism claims more reliably than causal performance claims.
4. A proposal becomes defensible when every subquestion has accessible evidence and a necessary method.
5. Version control, source verification, editable figures, and final layout review are part of research quality, not only document administration.
6. When an advisor asks for clearer Chapters 3–6, revise the question–chapter mapping first; prose editing should follow the new architecture.

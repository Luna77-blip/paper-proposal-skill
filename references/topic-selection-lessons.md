# Topic Selection Lessons from Rejected and Redirected Candidates

Read this file when the user has several rejected topics, is unsure why a seemingly interesting case failed, or needs to redirect a narrow idea without discarding useful evidence.

The examples below are anonymized archetypes derived from real proposal iterations. They preserve the decision logic while removing identifying details.

## 1. Key-audit-matter text around an accounting restatement

### Initial idea

A consumer-products company had experienced an accounting restatement, repeated exchange inquiries, historical operating distress, and several years of revenue-recognition key audit matters. The proposed paper examined whether key audit matter disclosure changed after the restatement.

### Why it was rejected

- The observable audit text was limited and often standardized.
- The central question risked becoming a description of wording changes.
- Public evidence could not reveal the auditor's internal risk assessment or procedures beyond the report.
- The importance of the question to current audit practice was not established.
- The proposal initially concentrated on two restatement years and underused the longer regulatory and governance history.

### What could be salvaged

The longer timeline of inquiry letters, distress, accounting correction, audit response, and governance change could support a broader study only if the research problem moved beyond text similarity. Possible redirections include:

- how repeated regulatory inquiries interact with accounting correction and governance response;
- whether audit communication absorbs newly revealed risks, using carefully bounded evidence;
- how revenue-recognition controls change after regulatory intervention, if control evidence is available.

### Decision rule

If the only reliable evidence is repeated audit-report wording, keep the analysis as a section or reject the topic. Promote it to a thesis only when a broader, observable governance or regulatory process exists.

## 2. Asset-management expansion and earnings quality

### Initial idea

A healthcare manufacturer accumulated extraordinary cash from a temporary operating boom and subsequently expanded financial-asset investment. Fair-value changes and investment income became material to profit, while operating revenue weakened.

### Why it was considered risky

- “Profit sources and earnings quality” was broad and widely studied.
- The initial title did not identify a sufficiently important accounting judgment.
- A generic financialization-performance framework would closely resemble existing case studies.
- Private investment valuations and underlying portfolio performance were difficult to verify.
- Claims about investment effectiveness could not be independently tested.

### What could be salvaged

The case became more defensible when tied to a precise accounting or disclosure problem, such as:

- fair-value measurement and the interpretation of earnings quality;
- classification of investment gains as recurring or non-recurring;
- information content of adjusted profit measures after a business-model change;
- audit response to Level 3 valuation uncertainty.

### Decision rule

Do not use “financialization,” “cross-industry investment,” or “asset allocation effect” as the central question unless the evidence supports independent evaluation. Prefer a specific accounting judgment, measurement change, or information-quality problem.

## 3. Scope 3 boundary restatement and comparability

### Initial idea

A global manufacturer substantially restated its base-year value-chain emissions after organizational and calculation-boundary changes. The initial topic focused on whether the disclosure adequately explained the restatement and preserved comparability.

### Why it was judged too small

- Boundary restatement was a technical point rather than a complete organizational problem.
- Public evidence could reveal the restatement but not fully decompose every numerical driver.
- The likely conclusion—disclosure was incomplete—was correct but insufficient for a full master's thesis.
- The topic underused evidence on strategy, management control, supplier governance, and global operations.

### What could be salvaged

The restatement became the technical axis of a broader governance study:

- external low-carbon rules and public customer requirements;
- internal management control and carbon-data systems;
- Scope 3 boundaries, factors, supplier data, and base-year restatement;
- supplier admission, audit, remediation, and capability building;
- support for customer response and overseas compliance preparation.

### Decision rule

When a narrow measurement issue is evidence-rich and connects several organizational processes, retain it as a core chapter or technical axis. Do not force it to carry the entire thesis.

## 4. ESG committee establishment as a governance topic

### Initial idea

A listed company created a board-level ESG committee and received external recognition for sustainability governance.

### Typical rejection risk

- Committee establishment alone is an organizational fact, not a research problem.
- Awards and best-practice labels may create a promotional narrative.
- Public materials may show structure but not decision use or governance consequences.
- The topic can collapse into an ESG-report summary.

### Better direction

Trace how board oversight changes responsibility, data ownership, control procedures, investment decisions, risk reporting, or assurance. The committee should be an explanatory mechanism, not the outcome being celebrated.

## 5. Venture-capital or cross-industry investment “effect”

### Initial idea

A non-financial listed company invested through funds, direct equity, and early-stage technology projects. The proposed study asked whether the investment model created value.

### Typical rejection risk

- Underlying project performance and valuation inputs are not public.
- Unrealized gains cannot establish long-term investment effectiveness.
- A single successful valuation date creates survivorship and timing bias.
- The paper may reproduce company narratives instead of conducting independent analysis.

### Better direction

If evidence is sufficient, examine financial-instrument classification, fair-value hierarchy, disclosure verifiability, recurring-profit judgment, or audit procedures. Otherwise, keep the investment path as background rather than the thesis outcome.

## 6. Regulatory inquiry letters and “regulatory effect”

### Initial idea

A company received repeated exchange inquiries, followed by accounting correction or governance change.

### Typical rejection risk

- Temporal sequence does not prove regulatory effect.
- Multiple inquiries may concern unrelated issues.
- Later improvement may reflect business recovery, management change, or other regulation.
- A single case lacks a credible counterfactual for strong causal claims.

### Better direction

Study the response mechanism, issue continuity, information correction process, or governance adaptation. Use “regulatory response” or “governance process after inquiry” unless the design can identify an effect.

## 7. Policy-slogan topics

### Initial idea

A title combines a recent policy phrase with “path,” “effect,” or “value creation.”

### Why it is weak

- The policy phrase substitutes for a research construct.
- The intended effect is undefined or unmeasurable.
- The case is selected because it fits a slogan rather than because it reveals a mechanism.

### Better direction

Translate the policy into an observable institutional change, accounting treatment, incentive, constraint, control, or decision problem. Use the policy as context unless it changes the mechanism being studied.

## 8. Case substitution mistaken for novelty

### Initial idea

An existing framework is applied to a new company with a similar event.

### Why it is weak

The paper changes the company name but not the assumptions, mechanism, data, or theoretical boundary.

### Better direction

Identify at least one meaningful increment:

- a new institutional rule;
- a new accounting judgment;
- a different governance role;
- longitudinal evidence unavailable in prior work;
- a boundary change hidden in aggregate data;
- a case that challenges an assumption in existing literature.

## How to redirect a rejected topic

Use the following sequence:

1. **Locate the rejection type**: importance, scope, evidence, method, discipline, or duplication.
2. **Identify the strongest retained evidence**: do not discard verified data merely because the title failed.
3. **Raise or lower the analytical level**:
   - too narrow → embed the issue in a broader mechanism;
   - too broad → isolate one accounting judgment or governance process;
   - effect not identifiable → study process, information quality, or decision use;
   - weak discipline fit → reconnect the case to measurement, control, assurance, incentives, or governance.
4. **Rewrite the main question before rewriting the title**.
5. **Re-run P0 and P1** because a redirected question changes the evidence requirement.

## Preferred topic directions

Promising proposal directions often involve one or more of the following:

- a recent accounting, auditing, disclosure, or governance rule that changes practice;
- a material accounting judgment whose information consequences are observable;
- a longitudinal governance process with multiple evidence points;
- a measurement-boundary or data-governance problem with decision relevance;
- an external requirement that becomes an internal control or management-accounting mechanism;
- a core firm's ability to transmit controls across organizational boundaries;
- a strategic transformation that changes how accounting information should be interpreted;
- a conflict between reported performance, cash flow, valuation, control, or business narrative.

The strongest direction is not necessarily the newest. It is the one where an important question, a revealing case, accessible evidence, and an executable method overlap.

## Reject, narrow, or retain as a module

| Situation | Decision |
|---|---|
| Central evidence is inaccessible and no substitute exists | Reject |
| Question is common but a precise accounting judgment is observable | Narrow and redirect |
| Technical issue is too small but connects a broader mechanism | Retain as a chapter or analytical axis |
| Case is new but mechanism duplicates prior work | Reject or find a real increment |
| Effect cannot be identified but process is observable | Reframe as mechanism or response study |
| Advisor rejects discipline fit | Reconnect to measurement, control, assurance, incentives, or governance; otherwise reject |
